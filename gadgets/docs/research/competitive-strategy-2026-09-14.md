# gadgets.sh: competitive research and business direction

Research date: September 14, 2026. Status: recommendation and experiments, not validated demand or a revenue forecast.

## Recommendation

Build a specialist publication and small hardware business for people who love **hackable handhelds and radio gear**. Make original demonstrations the entry point. Give each compelling project enough tested detail to reproduce it, then offer the exact parts or a supported kit when fulfillment is viable. Use what customers actually build and buy to inform LilyShark's original hardware.

The proposed promise is: **Find something remarkable to do with pocket hardware. See it work. Make it yours.**

This preserves the founder's interest in discovery and people sharing their own builds. The commercial product is a useful working setup, access to original hardware, or a concrete service for a maker. A project profile is the record that connects them.

The central uncertainty is whether our original projects are sufficiently desirable and useful that experienced hobbyists choose them over existing videos, project sites and official documentation. A redesign cannot settle that. Test three excellent projects and one commercial offer before building a larger social platform.

### Three conclusions from the comparison

1. **Our previous differentiation was overstated.** Creator profiles, collections, videos, parts lists and sharing are already available in several combinations. Adafruit even documents hardware-revision substitutions in existing guides. [Revision-aware example](https://learn.adafruit.com/led-matrix-hourglass?view=all).
2. **Revenue follows a concrete customer.** A reader purchases hardware; a maker purchases content or promotion; a seller purchases launch/distribution services. Community activity can support those transactions, but does not automatically create them.
3. **We need material that is difficult to reproduce by summarizing other pages.** Original firmware, photographed experiments, reproducible test results, precise compatibility records, maintained instructions and relationships with makers could become assets. We do not yet have evidence that their combination is a competitive advantage.

## What this means for the UI

The current local preview leads with a large wordmark, an editorial invitation and a setup editor. It asks visitors to understand our platform and contribute before seeing enough exceptional material. That is a product-design judgment based on the implementation and the user's repeated rejection, not a measured conversion result.

The next design should be built around real content with this hierarchy:

1. **A working object immediately.** A short, controllable demonstration or excellent photograph, a specific outcome, and the maker's identity. Show several discoveries within easy reach. A sample headline might describe turning a handheld into a field instrument; publish it only after demonstrating the actual capability.
2. **Proof close to the promise.** Hardware revision, firmware version, test conditions, known limitations and whether a statement comes from our test or the maker. The catalog's source review is not equivalent to hands-on testing.
3. **A clear next action.** Open the build, use hardware already owned, or see the exact parts and their dated cost. Offer a complete kit only when there is an actual offer we can fulfill.
4. **Depth on demand.** Code, wiring, installation, alternatives and troubleshooting belong in the project. An expert should reach them directly without a beginner onboarding sequence.
5. **An earned invitation to participate.** After the demonstration, invite the visitor to show a modification or completed version. Preserve credit and link back to the source project.

Use real hardware photography and demonstrations as the visual identity. A compact navigation area, readable technical detail and enough density to browse are stronger starting requirements than a new font or color theme. Short clips can introduce a project without forcing every visitor into an endless video feed.

Candidate navigation: **Discover · Builds · Hardware**. A shop becomes prominent when it has real products. This is a testable information hierarchy, not another completed design.

## The first product to test

### Audience and job

Experienced hobbyists who already own, modify or are considering a hackable handheld: “Show me something this device can actually do that makes it worth my time or money.” Start with radio tools and pocket computers close to the founder's existing work. Expand only when the same format works elsewhere.

### One complete project page

- A short original demonstration with the result visible before a long explanation.
- Creator credit and links to the original repository or build log.
- Exact board, revision, accessories and firmware release/commit; date last reproduced.
- Reproduction steps and a downloadable configuration or code artifact where appropriate.
- Observed limits, failed approaches and a concise troubleshooting section.
- Dated parts list: required, optional and already owned; identify tested substitutions.
- A purchase route with merchant/stock/currency context. Initially separate merchant links; do not suggest there is a unified checkout.
- A completion or modification report that captures what the next builder changed and whether it worked.

First subject candidates are a LilyShark workflow on the actual T-Deck development hardware, a useful pocket-computer project, and a small radio setup with a measurable outcome. Choose only projects we can physically reproduce. These are editorial candidates, not claims that the current prototype implements or verifies them. Keep the future original LilyShark device labeled as a concept.

### Why someone might share it

The shareable unit is **a surprising result with an identifiable maker**, accompanied by a link that answers “how did you do that?” The maker gets credit, a durable record and interested visitors. A recipient can reproduce or adapt it. A successful adaptation produces another demonstration and a link back to the original.

This is a growth hypothesis. It needs actual creator participation, original media, public URLs and usable sharing previews. A Share button or an encoded URL does not establish distribution. Creator-controlled publication, an opt-in digest and search pages about specific device capabilities are proposed channels; no outreach, subscriptions or posts have been made.

Returning owners should see new projects for hardware they already have, changes to builds they use, and useful answers to implementation problems. Those are candidate reasons to return that should be measured separately from novelty clicks.

## How to make money

### Recommended order

| Route | Customer and deliverable | When it makes sense | Main constraint |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Affiliate purchases** | Merchant pays for an attributable eligible completed order. | Early, once accepted into relevant programs and useful project pages exist. | Small commission per order; not all products, merchants or publishers qualify. |
| **Technical production for makers** | A hardware maker buys a working demo, tutorial, photos and reusable launch assets. | An early paid pilot can test demand without promising a large audience. | Labor-intensive service revenue. Publish commissioned work with clear attribution; editorial approval is separate. |
| **Curated kits / original accessories** | A buyer pays for the convenience and support of a tested setup. | After repeat requests, actual supplier quotes and a supportable small-batch offer. | Inventory, quality control, fulfillment, returns and support consume margin. |
| **Original LilyShark hardware** | A buyer pays for a demonstrated capability and usable physical product. | After the defining workflow, buyer demand and manufacturing economics are validated. | Engineering and launch costs; gadgets.sh traffic does not prove product demand. |
| **Sponsorship / paid promotion** | A brand purchases measured access to relevant buyers. | Once audience composition and commercial outcomes can be shown. | An empty audience has little exposure value. Gadget Flow's rate card is not our starting price. |

Basic discovery and project reading should remain free during validation. A membership needs a recurring paid benefit, and a marketplace needs active buyers and sellers. Neither has been demonstrated here.

### Affiliate programs actually checked

**Seeed Studio:** published base commission is **3% on eligible products**, with 45-day attribution and higher rates for certain campaigns. Hardware rates must not be confused with its separate 20% software-subscription campaign. Only completed qualifying orders earn commission; acceptance and terms apply. [Program](https://www.seeedstudio.com/blog/affiliate-program/), [terms](https://www.seeedstudio.com/amasty-affiliate-conditions).

**CircuitMess:** an official Awin merchant profile offers a program with a 30-day attribution period; this review did not establish a public commission percentage. Its terms require approval and restrict unauthorized sub-affiliate arrangements. [Merchant profile](https://ui.awin.com/merchant-profile/124978), [terms](https://ui.awin.com/merchant-profile-terms/124978).

The local 45-record catalog contains one Seeed entry and seven CircuitMess entries. That is a manufacturer match, **not confirmation that these eight products are currently commissionable**. No applications or affiliate accounts were created. Do not assume creator revenue-sharing is automatically allowed by a merchant program; a direct creator production/licensing agreement is a separate model.

### Illustrative economics — assumptions, not forecasts

Affiliate revenue is:

`visits × share clicking to merchant × merchant conversion × eligible basket value × commission`

The table assumes 20% click-through, 3% merchant conversion, a $140 eligible basket and a 3% commission. Only the base commission has a current published program precedent; traffic, click-through, conversion, basket size and product eligibility are hypothetical. It assumes successful attribution and no reversals.

| Monthly visits | Merchant clicks | Completed orders | Referred merchandise sales | Gross commission |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 10,000 | 2,000 | 60 | $8,400 | **$252** |
| 50,000 | 10,000 | 300 | $42,000 | **$1,260** |
| 100,000 | 20,000 | 600 | $84,000 | **$2,520** |

At these assumptions, commission is 2.52 cents per site visit. Recovering $1,000 of content cost requires roughly 39,683 visits before any other expenses. Halving conversion halves revenue. Referred merchandise sales are the merchant's sales, not gadgets.sh revenue. These figures explain why attention and a viable business are different milestones.

An illustrative $149 kit might incur $82 landed hardware cost, $12 assembly/QA/packing, $5 payment fees, $8 shipping subsidy and $10 returns/support reserve. That leaves **$32 contribution per order**, or about 21.5% of price, before customer acquisition, development, overhead and taxes. One hundred orders yield $3,200 contribution, not profit; $15 acquisition cost per order would reduce it to $1,700. Every cost here needs a quote or measured first-batch result.

A technical-content pilot priced experimentally at $1,500 would produce $3,000 revenue from two clients. At 20 hours per client and an assumed $50/hour labor cost, $1,000 remains before samples, travel, equipment and overhead. This tests service demand; it is not passive income or evidence of a scalable platform. No prospective client has accepted this price.

For own hardware, compare direct sales with established channels using full costs. Crowd Supply's typical campaign fee is not its total fee, and fulfillment varies. [Fulfillment scope](https://www.crowdsupply.com/guide/fulfillment-and-logistics). Seeed also advertises a licensing/manufacturing route with negotiated royalties; it is an option to investigate, not an accepted partnership. [Licensed program](https://www.seeedstudio.com/blog/licensed-program/).

## A 30-day validation plan

These are proposed experiments, not work already performed. Timings assume access to hardware and a small group of participants.

| Period | Work | Evidence required before expanding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Days 1–7 | Select three feasible outcomes. Observe 10 hobbyists trying to find and reproduce comparable projects using their normal resources. Record where existing sites already work well. | A repeated problem we can materially reduce; enough original footage and working hardware to make one page credible. |
| Days 8–14 | Produce three complete project pages, starting with one actual LilyShark development workflow. Record time, parts cost and failures. Test discovery and reproduction with participants. | Visitors understand the demonstrated result; builders can reproduce it with the specified revisions. |
| Days 15–21 | Test one real commercial offer: a quoted, supportable small kit batch or a clearly scoped technical-production pilot. Add eligible affiliate links only after acceptance. | Genuine paid demand or attributable orders; record delivery labor and support time. Interest clicks remain a weaker signal. |
| Days 22–30 | Let willing creators share the completed work through their own channels. Offer an opt-in digest of new projects. Review referrals, repeat visits, build outcomes and contribution. | Independent sharing and useful downstream actions across several projects, plus a plausible paid path. |

Proposed decision gates, chosen for this experiment rather than presented as industry benchmarks:

- At least 8 of 10 target testers can explain the featured capability and locate exact requirements without guidance.
- At least 4 of 5 reproduction testers finish one deliberately bounded workflow with no more than one clarification, under recorded conditions.
- At least three independent makers voluntarily share or submit a meaningful adaptation; inspect whether recipients take a useful action.
- Obtain either three paid orders for a fulfillable pilot kit or one paid production pilot before investing in those routes. These are initial signals, not proof of product-market fit.
- Require positive measured contribution on the pilot, including direct support labor. Record fixed costs separately.

Track demonstration opens, parts clicks, saved projects, build starts/completions, voluntary referrals, repeat use by device owners, attributable paid orders, support time and contribution. Break results down by project and referral source. A popular clip with no useful follow-through should not justify a full platform build.

## Immediate implementation scope after research

Design **one discovery screen and one excellent project page** around actual media. Test them before extending the visual system. Public hosting, durable IDs and crawler-readable sharing previews are necessary for a real sharing experiment. Authentication, upload infrastructure and public submissions become necessary when outside creators need durable ownership and editing; they do not all have to precede the first editorial page.

Preserve the catalog as supporting reference. Its 44 archive entries are useful initial data, but not the source of a durable competitive advantage. Keep the distinction between maker-sourced claims and independently tested results. The original-device brief remains relevant; product demand learned through this process should revise it.

## Method and limits

Reviewed public homepages, concrete project pages, disclosures, pricing and official program terms. Live Arc inspection supplements the source research; see [visual observations](visual-observations-2026-09-14.md). No private analytics, audited revenue or profitability data was obtained. Marketing audience/ROI claims were not used to size the opportunity.

PCPartPicker returned access errors through the research fetcher; its current product and commercial details were not verified. Rebrickable's help page was inaccessible; its row relies on an indexed primary project page. Tindie's current fee schedule remains unverified. Some Everyday Carry pages were available as indexed primary-source text but returned 403 on direct fetch. ThisIsWhyImBroke's fetch output contained unresolved JavaScript templates; those placeholders are not evidence of the rendered interface or an authenticated admin account.

No application UI changes, deployments, commercial signups, purchases, outreach or external posts were made for this research. The existing local preview is an interaction prototype, not a functioning hosted community or commerce business.
